Reading a water meter I’m not allowed to touch

7 November 2026 · Uladzimir Kazakevich · 19 min read

A camera board, a 3D-printed bracket that had to stay clear of the utility’s sealed radio, and a neural network that can’t read the digit 5.

This is the main water meter in my garage. The white thing tied to it is mine: a small camera in a 3D-printed bracket that reads the digits.

The bracket has this odd shape because of what was on the meter first:

The brass body and the white collar are the meter. The grey ring and the grey box with the wire belong to the city’s water utility: a radio that reports my consumption to them. It is sealed, and I’m not allowed to touch it. They get my readings by radio. I get a bill.

I wanted the same numbers in Home Assistant, the open-source system that runs my house. So I gave Claude three photos and one line:

I have that water meter. I would like to have its measurements in HA

A Friday evening and a Saturday morning later, about eight hours in all, the meter was on my energy dashboard, read every two minutes. On the way there: two dead ends, a plan that lasted an hour, a confident “no” that one more photo turned into a “yes”, a bracket designed around a box it may not touch, and a neural network that keeps reading 5 as 4.

This is the whole story with the wrong turns left in, because they are the useful part.

The short version

  • The problem. The utility’s module is sealed and encrypted, and gives the owner of the house nothing. It also covers part of the meter’s window.
  • What works. A small ESP32-CAM board looks at the digits from the one side the module leaves open. The open-source firmware AI-on-the-edge recognises them on the board itself and sends the number to Home Assistant.
  • The catch. The network that recognises the digits reads this meter’s 5 as a 4, again and again.
  • The fix. Thirty lines of template in Home Assistant know that only a 4 can be a hidden 5. They repair the reading and send the right value back to the camera.

Who did what. I built this with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, working as an agent: it has my home-automation git repository, a shell, a CAD program and a browser. I was the hands. Photos, calipers, the printer, the mounting and every password were mine. The quoted prompts are mine, the quoted answers are Claude’s.

Why not just ask the box on top?

Claude named the parts from my photos: a Diehl Altair V4 meter, and on it an AIUT APULSE-W, the radio module my utility (MPWiK, in Wrocław, Poland) fits to its meters. Then it went through what that module could give me.

Nothing. The module picks up the meter’s rotation without any contact and sends encrypted messages over the operator’s own city-wide radio network. It has no pulse output, no NFC and no optical port. It treats a magnet as tampering. And it is sealed. That set the rule for everything after: nothing may touch the module, its ring or its seals.

The second idea was the utility’s customer website, which shows the readings that module sends. Someone has written a Home Assistant integration for it. But the login is protected by a CAPTCHA, so the integration lives on a browser cookie you copy over by hand, and the numbers arrive two to three days late. That is no use for a leak alert. We wrote it down as “official numbers to compare with, maybe later” and never installed it.

The plan that lasted an hour

Claude’s first real recommendation involved no camera at all. Inside the meter a small magnet turns with the water. A tiny magnetometer chip (QMC5883L) strapped to the meter body can count the turns, and there is a ready ESPHome project for exactly that. No optics, nothing near the module. Claude wrote a draft configuration the same evening.

It was parked within the hour, because I remembered something in a drawer.

“It won’t work.” Then one more photo.

I had once tried to point an ESP32-CAM at this meter. That device was last seen on my network in September 2024. I asked whether the idea deserved another try (MPWiK is the utility; the “huge thing” is its grey box):

esp32-watermeter it was esp32 + camera (built-in) attempt to read the numbers. I think it was - https://github.com/jomjol/AI-on-the-edge-device can that work? MPWiK is huge thing and distracts a bit the view, not fully

Claude looked at my first photo and said no:

AI-on-the-edge would work mechanically, but not usefully: the MPWiK clamp hides the digits that matter.

In that photo it is right. The last three wheels, the red ones that count litres, sit under the module:

Without them the reading moves once per cubic metre, which here means one tick every two or three days. But the answer ended with a line I’m glad it wrote: “One check before I close this door”. Look from the other side, away from the module. I went back to the garage.

All eight digits, 535.508 m³. The next reply began with “These photos change the verdict”.

The lesson cost nothing and I’ll keep it: one photo proves what is visible from one angle, and nothing more. Before ruling something out, take the second angle.

A bracket for a meter with a box in the way

Claude designs parts in a parametric CAD program that it drives itself, and I print them. I asked for a model.

The obvious place to hang a bracket is the grey ring around the window. That idea was dropped at once: the ring is part of the utility’s module. Under it sits the white ribbed collar, which belongs to the meter. So version 1 is a saddle that leans against that collar and is held by one zip tie, with an arm that holds the camera 70 mm from the digits, tilted 25°.

It was never printed. Before spending filament I sent Claude a list of YouTube videos and published models of AI-on-the-edge housings and asked:

look at what they print and why, solving a similar problem. […] Improve your design but so that it fits my case. The ESP32-cam also has a built-in LED light - at night the garage is dark

Almost everybody prints the same thing: a closed, dark tube that sits on the meter, with the camera at the top looking straight down. Their reasons are good ones:

  • The tube shuts out room light, so the picture is the same day and night.
  • The distance is fixed, so focus and the digit regions stay valid.
  • The LED’s reflection in the glass can be controlled.
  • The board is protected, and the SD card stays reachable.

A tube can’t go round my meter. The module covers half the ring. So version 2 copies the reasons, not the shape:

The check that saved a print

The CAD model can render what the lens will see. It said the red digits were hidden:

But the module in the model is a plain box, and the real one is stepped. So before printing I held my phone roughly where the lens would be:

All eight digits, with about 2 mm to spare next to the module. No change to the model. It was printed on a Bambu Lab P1S and on the meter before 11 that morning.

The picture at the top of the post shows the whole thing. Up close: the saddle leans on the white collar, two zip ties joined into one loop hold it, and nothing is fastened to the utility’s grey ring.

First light

The second session started with:

I printed and installed the watermeter esp32-cam. […] use Google Chrome […] to set up the watermeter. and let me know if something needs fixing.

AI-on-the-edge is set up in a web page served by the camera itself. Claude operated that page through a browser. I signed in myself; it never typed the password. The first frame:

Sideways and soft, but all eight wheels are in the picture. The bracket worked. From here on it was tuning, and nearly every step went wrong once before it went right.

The tilt. Rotating by 90° looked almost right. I said the row of digits was still slanted. Instead of nudging by eye, Claude measured the slope of the window’s edge in pixels: 9.6° off at −90°, 5.2° at −94.5°, 0.0° at −99.5°. It stayed at −99.5°.

The garbage. You tell the firmware where each digit is by drawing a rectangle on the picture. The first rectangles were 42 × 68 pixels, then 36 × 58. The readings were nonsense: 1042N.N36, 10131.0N7, 00111.119. The very first reading the device accepted put 10,434.836 m³ on a meter that stood at 535.

The fix came from looking at what the network actually receives. The firmware can save the cut-out digits:

Seen from 30°, this meter’s digits are about half as wide as the network expects, and the short rectangles cut their tops off. A 5, a 3 and an 8 without a top all look like a 1. Narrow, tall rectangles of 32 × 76 pixels fixed it.

The models. AI-on-the-edge ships several digit networks. Four were tried. One read every 5 as 4.3. One gave up on the 5 in two places out of three. The one that stayed (dig-cont_0900_s3_q) reads it as about 4.9, which sounds close enough. It isn’t, as the next section shows.

The focus problem that wasn’t. Half-way through, Claude noticed the picture had become softer and began to suspect the lens. My answer:

I just switched off the main light in the garage. didn’t change anything else

The network that can’t read a 5

With everything tuned, the camera read the right value. A few minutes later it read 101 m³ less. Then the right value again. For the next hour it kept jumping between four numbers, while two litres of water went through the meter.

The meter stood at 535.864 m³, a number with two 5s in front of the decimal point. In its first 72 minutes in Home Assistant the camera’s reading was right 41 % of the time. For the rest, one 5 or both came out as 4.

The digit network doesn’t answer “5”. It answers with a decimal, because a wheel can be half-way between two digits, and a clean 5 on this meter scores between 4.5 and 4.9. The firmware then has to make a whole digit out of that, and the result flips between 4 and 5 from one photo to the next.

The firmware does protect itself. A reading lower than the last one is rejected, so none of this reached the total. But while a 5 reads as 4, the value stands still and catches up later in one jump. A leak alert built on that would be worthless.

And I couldn’t fix it on the device from outside. Over MQTT it accepts two commands: set the previous value, and start a new reading. I asked three questions in a row:

can we, if there is a problem on the edge device, recognise it on the HA side and correct the values on the edge too?

can we set not only PreValue but the current value too?

or is it better that HA does the whole recognition?

The answers were: yes, but that alone won’t cure the misread. No. And better not: reading the photo in Home Assistant means either a cloud vision model called 720 times a day or one more local service to look after, and this error is narrow and always the same.

A rule instead of a bigger model

The camera’s usual mistake has a precise shape: a 5 turns into a 4. So the repair can be a rule. On every reading from the camera, Home Assistant does this:

  1. Find the wheels that read 4. Only those can be a misread 5.
  2. Try adding 1 to every combination of them.
  3. Keep the smallest result that lies between the last good value and the most water that could have flowed since (0.2 m³ plus 30 litres per minute).
  4. If nothing fits, keep the last value.

The heart of it is four lines:

{# Only a drum that reads 4 can be a misread 5: try +1 on every subset of those drums, keep the smallest fit #}
{% set c = (raw + acc.d) | round(3) %}
{% if c >= last - 0.003 and c <= last + max_up and (ns.best is none or c < ns.best) %}
  {% set ns.best = c %}
{% endif %}

The first version had a bug of its own: it could add 1 to any digit, not only to a 4. Claude caught that before it went live, and ran 12 test cases through Home Assistant’s template API, which only evaluates a template and changes nothing.

A small automation closes the loop. When the camera’s stored previous value drifts more than 5 litres from the repaired one, Home Assistant sends the good value back. The camera can’t stay stuck on a wrong number. In the chart above, the bottom bar is the result: one flat line that moved by 2 litres when 2 litres flowed.

The whole Home Assistant package (template sensor + automation)
template:
  - trigger:
      - trigger: state
        entity_id: sensor.watermeter_raw
        not_to: [unknown, unavailable]
      # an unchanged read doesn't fire the state trigger; also seeds the sensor after a reload
      - trigger: time_pattern
        minutes: /5
    sensor:
      - name: Water meter
        unique_id: water_meter_total
        unit_of_measurement: m³
        device_class: water
        state_class: total_increasing
        icon: mdi:water
        state: >
          {% set rawstr = states('sensor.watermeter_raw') %}
          {% set raw = rawstr | float(none) %}
          {% set last = this.state | float(none) %}
          {% if last is none %}
            {% set last = (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') | float(none) %}
          {% endif %}
          {% if raw is none or last is none %}
            {{ last if last is not none else this.state }}
          {% else %}
            {% set mins = ((now() - this.last_changed).total_seconds() / 60) if this.last_changed is defined else 2 %}
            {% set max_up = 0.2 + 0.03 * mins %}
            {# Only a drum that reads 4 can be a misread 5: try +1 on every subset of those drums, keep the smallest fit #}
            {% set digits = rawstr | replace('.', '') %}
            {% set weights = [10000, 1000, 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001] %}
            {% set ns = namespace(pos=[], best=none) %}
            {% for i in range([digits | length, weights | length] | min) %}
              {% if digits[i] == '4' %}{% set ns.pos = ns.pos + [weights[i]] %}{% endif %}
            {% endfor %}
            {% for m in range(2 ** (ns.pos | length)) %}
              {% set acc = namespace(d=0) %}
              {% for k in range(ns.pos | length) %}
                {% if (m // (2 ** k)) % 2 == 1 %}{% set acc.d = acc.d + ns.pos[k] %}{% endif %}
              {% endfor %}
              {% set c = (raw + acc.d) | round(3) %}
              {% if c >= last - 0.003 and c <= last + max_up and (ns.best is none or c < ns.best) %}
                {% set ns.best = c %}
              {% endif %}
            {% endfor %}
            {{ [ns.best, last] | max if ns.best is not none else last }}
          {% endif %}
        attributes:
          raw: "{{ states('sensor.watermeter_raw') }}"
          device_pre: "{{ (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') }}"

automation:
  - id: water_meter_sync_prevalue
    alias: "Water meter: keep the camera's PreValue in step"
    mode: single
    trigger:
      - trigger: state
        entity_id: sensor.watermeter_json
    variables:
      pre: "{{ (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') | float(none) }}"
      good: "{{ states('sensor.water_meter') | float(none) }}"
    condition:
      - "{{ pre is not none and good is not none and (pre - good) | abs > 0.005 }}"
    action:
      - action: mqtt.publish
        data:
          topic: watermeter/ctrl/set_prevalue
          payload: '{"numbersname": "main", "value": {{ good }}}'

sensor.watermeter_raw and sensor.watermeter_json are created by AI-on-the-edge through MQTT discovery; watermeter is the device’s name and main the name of the number in its config.

Building hardware with an AI: an honest scorecard

What Claude did well

  • Named the meter and the radio module from three phone photos, and found out what the module can and can’t do before I wasted time on it.
  • Ended its wrong verdict with the one check that could overturn it.
  • Read up on why other people’s housings look the way they do, then kept the reasons and dropped the shape.
  • Measured instead of guessing: the tilt in pixels, the digit cut-outs instead of the pretty full frame.
  • Tested the template on 12 cases without touching the live system, and caught its own bug before it went live.

What it got wrong

  • “It won’t work”, from one photo taken at the worst angle.
  • A measuring list in CAD jargon, for someone holding calipers in a garage.
  • A module drawn as a plain box, so its own simulation said the digits were hidden.
  • A suspected focus problem that was me switching the light off.

What I had to bring

  • The second angle, the fact that the meter can be turned, and the remark that the row was still slanted. All three are things you only know standing in front of the meter.
  • The push to look at other people’s designs before printing the first idea.
  • The question whether Home Assistant could repair what the device couldn’t.

And the boring failures. Claude couldn’t send a picture into our chat, so it pushed the file to the repository and gave me a link. One model site answered its fetch with an error page, so it read the pages through a real browser. For the videos it could only get titles, descriptions and chapter lists, and said so. I ran into my usage limit in the middle of the tuning. A browser tab froze on a dialog. And once I typed “it hung?” when it was only waiting two minutes for the camera to reboot.

If you want to build one

Parts

  • An ESP32-CAM (AI-Thinker type, OV2640 camera, 4 MB flash, PSRAM) with the ESP32-CAM-MB USB base, a microSD card and a micro-USB cable to a USB power supply.
  • A printed mount. Mine weighs about 46 g by the model and fits exactly one combination of meter and module, so it is no use to you as a file. The idea carries over: hang on a part that belongs to the meter, look from the side the module leaves open, shade the glass, and tilt the camera so the LED’s reflection misses the digits.
  • Home Assistant with an MQTT broker (I use the Mosquitto add-on).

What I’d tell myself before starting

  • One photo is one angle. Take the second before deciding.
  • Ask for measuring instructions as a picture, in plain words.
  • Look at what others built, then find the constraint of yours that breaks their design. Copy the reasons, not the shape.
  • Hold a phone where the lens will be before you print.
  • Look at the cut-outs the network gets, not at the nice full frame.
  • Measure the tilt. Don’t eyeball it.
  • Before diagnosing a change in the picture, ask what changed in the room.
  • A narrow, repeatable error at the edge is cheaper to repair with a rule downstream than by replacing the recogniser.

What’s next

  • An alert for water running at night, or for a leak.
  • An alert when the camera goes offline. The device’s own “problem” flag is raised by every misread 5, so it can’t be used.
  • Daily and monthly counters.
  • A sharper image. A refocused lens would probably make the 5 reliable and leave the repair rule with nothing to do.
  • One comparison with the utility’s own numbers.
