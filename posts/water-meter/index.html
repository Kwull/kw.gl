Reading a water meter I’m not allowed to touch

7 November 2026 · Uladzimir Kazakevich · 14 min read

The white thing zip-tied to my water meter is a small camera in a 3D-printed bracket. It takes a photo of the digits every two minutes, and the reading goes to Home Assistant, the open-source system that runs my house.

From my first message to a live reading on the dashboard it took about eight hours of work, a Friday evening and a Saturday morning. In that time a custom part was designed, printed and mounted, the camera was set up, and the code was written.

I didn’t do that alone. I worked with Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, the way Tony Stark works with JARVIS in Iron Man. I said what I wanted, took photos and held the calipers. Claude did the research, the 3D model, the camera setup and the code. It ran as an agent with access to my home-automation repository, a shell, a CAD program and a browser.

The short version: the utility’s radio on top of the meter is useless to me, so a camera reads the digits from the one side that radio leaves free. The neural network that reads them keeps taking this meter’s 5 for a 4, and a short template in Home Assistant corrects that.

The meter already has a radio, but not for me

The brass body and the white collar are the meter. The grey ring and the grey box with the wire belong to my city’s water utility (MPWiK in Wrocław, Poland). It’s a radio module that reports my consumption to them. It is sealed, I’m not allowed to touch it, and it covers part of the meter’s window.

I wanted the same numbers at home, so on Friday at 20:14 I sent Claude three photos and one line:

I have that water meter. I would like to have its measurements in HA

Claude recognised the meter (a Diehl Altair V4) and the module (an AIUT APULSE-W) from the photos and looked up what the module could give me, which turned out to be nothing. It sends encrypted messages over the utility’s own radio network, has no output I could tap, and treats a magnet as tampering. From then on, nothing we tried was allowed to touch the module, its ring or its seals.

Two other ideas didn’t last long. The utility’s customer website shows these readings, but behind a CAPTCHA login and two or three days late, which doesn’t help if I want to hear about a leak the same day. A magnetometer chip strapped to the meter body could count the turns of a small magnet inside the meter, and there is a ready ESPHome project for that. Claude wrote a configuration for it, and we parked it within the hour, because I remembered something I had in a drawer.

Trying the camera again

I had once tried to read this meter with an ESP32-CAM, a small microcontroller board with Wi-Fi and a camera. With the open-source firmware AI-on-the-edge it photographs a meter, reads the digits with a small neural network on the board itself, and sends the number to Home Assistant over MQTT, a simple message protocol. That old device was last seen on my network in September 2024. I asked whether the idea deserved a second try.

Claude looked at my first photo and said no:

AI-on-the-edge would work mechanically, but not usefully: the MPWiK clamp hides the digits that matter.

In that photo it’s right. The last three wheels, the red ones that count litres, sit under the module, and without them the reading changes once per cubic metre, which in my house means every two or three days. But at the end of the same answer it asked me to check one thing before we closed that door: how the meter looks from the other side, away from the module. I went back to the garage.

From there you can see all eight digits, so at 20:41, not even half an hour after the first message, the camera was the plan.

The bracket

The camera needed something to hold it at a fixed distance, looking past the module. Claude designs parts in a CAD program that it drives itself, and I print them. Its first list of what to measure was in CAD jargon that I couldn’t follow, so I asked for it again in plain words and with pictures, and got arrows and numbers drawn on my own photo.

The obvious place to hang a bracket is the grey ring around the window, but that ring is part of the utility’s module. Below it is the white ribbed collar, which belongs to the meter, so the bracket leans against that and is held by a zip tie.

By 22:42 there was a first version, a simple open arm. I never printed it. Before spending filament I gave Claude a list of videos and published models of other people’s AI-on-the-edge housings and asked what they print and why. Almost all of them use a closed, dark tube that sits on the meter with the camera looking straight down. It keeps room light out, so the picture is the same day and night, and it fixes the distance.

A tube can’t go on my meter, because the module covers half of the ring. The second version, ready at 07:37 on Saturday, takes what it can from the idea: an arm, a visor and an end plate around the window, with the module itself as the fourth wall, and the camera tilted 30° so that reflections land away from the digits. It prints standing on the end plate, in one piece and without supports.

There was one more check before printing. The CAD model can render what the lens will see, and in that render the red digits were hidden behind the module. But the module in the model was a plain box and the real one is stepped, so I held my phone roughly where the lens would be and took a photo.

All eight digits were there, with about 2 mm to spare next to the module. I printed the bracket unchanged on my Bambu Lab P1S, and by 10:57 it was on the meter. It leans on the white collar, two zip ties joined into one loop hold it, and nothing is fastened to the utility’s grey ring.

Setting up the camera

AI-on-the-edge is configured in a web page that the camera serves itself. Claude worked in that page through a browser, after I had signed in. This was the first frame:

It’s sideways and a bit soft, but all eight wheels are in the picture, so the bracket did its job. What followed was tuning, and nearly every step went wrong once before it went right.

Rotating the image by 90° left the row of digits slightly slanted, so Claude measured the slope of the window’s edge in pixels and ended up at −99.5°.

Then you tell the firmware where each digit is by drawing a rectangle around it. With the first rectangles the readings were nonsense like 1042N.N36, and the very first value the device accepted was 10,434.836 m³ on a meter that stood at 535. What helped was looking at the small cut-outs the network actually receives, which the firmware can save:

Seen from 30°, this meter’s digits are about half as wide as the network expects, and my rectangles were too short and cut their tops off. A 5, a 3 and an 8 without a top all look like a 1. Narrow, tall rectangles of 32 × 76 pixels fixed that.

The firmware ships with several digit models. We tried four, and the one we kept reads this meter’s 5 as about 4.9, which sounded close enough at the time.

Somewhere in the middle of all this, Claude noticed that the picture had become softer and began to suspect the lens. I had to explain:

I just switched off the main light in the garage. didn’t change anything else

The 5 that reads as 4

At 11:58, an hour after I had mounted it, the camera read the right value. A few minutes later it read 101 m³ less, then the right value again, and for the next hour it kept jumping between four numbers while two litres of water went through the meter.

The meter stood at 535.864 m³, a number with two 5s in front of the decimal point. In its first 72 minutes the camera’s reading was right 41 % of the time, and the rest of the time one of those 5s or both came out as 4.

This happens because the network doesn’t answer with a digit. It answers with a decimal number, since a wheel can be half-way between two digits, and a clean 5 on this meter scores somewhere between 4.5 and 4.9. The firmware has to make a whole digit out of that, and it comes out as 4 on one photo and 5 on the next.

The firmware rejects a reading that is lower than the last one, so none of this reached the total. But while a 5 reads as 4, the value stands still and later catches up in one jump, and I can’t build a leak alert on that.

Fixing it in Home Assistant

Because the mistake is always a 5 that turns into a 4, Home Assistant can undo it with a rule. For every reading from the camera it takes the wheels that show a 4 and tries adding 1 to every combination of them. It keeps the smallest result that lies between the last good value and the most water that could have flowed since then (0.2 m³ plus 30 litres per minute). If nothing fits, it keeps the last value and waits for the next photo.

A small automation closes the loop. When the camera’s stored previous value is more than 5 litres away from the repaired one, Home Assistant sends the good value back, so the camera can’t stay stuck on a wrong number. The bottom bar in the chart above is the result: it stays flat, and it moved by 2 litres when 2 litres flowed.

Claude’s first version of the template could add 1 to any digit and not only to a 4. It caught that before anything went live and ran 12 test cases through Home Assistant’s template API, which evaluates a template and changes nothing. At 13:26 the corrected sensor was live and on my dashboard.

The whole Home Assistant package (template sensor + automation)
template:
  - trigger:
      - trigger: state
        entity_id: sensor.watermeter_raw
        not_to: [unknown, unavailable]
      # an unchanged read doesn't fire the state trigger; also seeds the sensor after a reload
      - trigger: time_pattern
        minutes: /5
    sensor:
      - name: Water meter
        unique_id: water_meter_total
        unit_of_measurement: m³
        device_class: water
        state_class: total_increasing
        icon: mdi:water
        state: >
          {% set rawstr = states('sensor.watermeter_raw') %}
          {% set raw = rawstr | float(none) %}
          {% set last = this.state | float(none) %}
          {% if last is none %}
            {% set last = (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') | float(none) %}
          {% endif %}
          {% if raw is none or last is none %}
            {{ last if last is not none else this.state }}
          {% else %}
            {% set mins = ((now() - this.last_changed).total_seconds() / 60) if this.last_changed is defined else 2 %}
            {% set max_up = 0.2 + 0.03 * mins %}
            {# Only a drum that reads 4 can be a misread 5: try +1 on every subset of those drums, keep the smallest fit #}
            {% set digits = rawstr | replace('.', '') %}
            {% set weights = [10000, 1000, 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001] %}
            {% set ns = namespace(pos=[], best=none) %}
            {% for i in range([digits | length, weights | length] | min) %}
              {% if digits[i] == '4' %}{% set ns.pos = ns.pos + [weights[i]] %}{% endif %}
            {% endfor %}
            {% for m in range(2 ** (ns.pos | length)) %}
              {% set acc = namespace(d=0) %}
              {% for k in range(ns.pos | length) %}
                {% if (m // (2 ** k)) % 2 == 1 %}{% set acc.d = acc.d + ns.pos[k] %}{% endif %}
              {% endfor %}
              {% set c = (raw + acc.d) | round(3) %}
              {% if c >= last - 0.003 and c <= last + max_up and (ns.best is none or c < ns.best) %}
                {% set ns.best = c %}
              {% endif %}
            {% endfor %}
            {{ [ns.best, last] | max if ns.best is not none else last }}
          {% endif %}
        attributes:
          raw: "{{ states('sensor.watermeter_raw') }}"
          device_pre: "{{ (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') }}"

automation:
  - id: water_meter_sync_prevalue
    alias: "Water meter: keep the camera's PreValue in step"
    mode: single
    trigger:
      - trigger: state
        entity_id: sensor.watermeter_json
    variables:
      pre: "{{ (states('sensor.watermeter_json') | from_json(default={})).get('pre') | float(none) }}"
      good: "{{ states('sensor.water_meter') | float(none) }}"
    condition:
      - "{{ pre is not none and good is not none and (pre - good) | abs > 0.005 }}"
    action:
      - action: mqtt.publish
        data:
          topic: watermeter/ctrl/set_prevalue
          payload: '{"numbersname": "main", "value": {{ good }}}'

sensor.watermeter_raw and sensor.watermeter_json are created by AI-on-the-edge through MQTT discovery; watermeter is the device’s name and main the name of the number in its config.

Claude as JARVIS

In the films, Tony Stark talks and JARVIS does the modelling. This weekend was close to that. Claude worked out what the utility’s module is and what it can’t do, designed the bracket in CAD, set up the camera through a browser and wrote the Home Assistant code. The suit may be the better comparison. I was still the one doing the project, but with it on, a custom printed part and working code fit into eight hours of a weekend.

It was wrong where it had only part of the picture. The “no” to the camera came from one photo taken at the worst angle, and its own simulation hid the digits because it had drawn the module as a plain box.

Some things only I could bring, because I was the one standing in front of the meter: the view from the other side, the slanted row, the light I had just switched off.

If you want to build one

You need an ESP32-CAM (AI-Thinker type, OV2640 camera, 4 MB flash, PSRAM) with the ESP32-CAM-MB USB base, a microSD card, a micro-USB cable with a USB power supply, and Home Assistant with an MQTT broker (I use the Mosquitto add-on).

You also need a printed mount. Mine fits exactly one combination of meter and module, so the file is no use to anybody else, but the idea carries over. Hang it on a part that belongs to the meter, look from the side the module leaves open, shade the glass, and tilt the camera so the LED’s reflection misses the digits. Before you print, hold a phone where the lens will be.
